# MTEC 3501 — Week 10 Assignment
## Progress Check-In, Feedback Response, and One-Week Sprint

**Class meeting:** November 5, 2026 (in person)  
**Due:** Before the Week 11 class meeting on November 12, 2026  
**Submission:** Updated project repository and GitHub Project

## Purpose

The mid-semester presentations gave you feedback while there is still time to use it. This week, turn that feedback and your current project state into a focused plan for the next step. You will share evidence of progress, identify what needs attention, and use a one-week sprint to organize achievable work in GitHub.

The sprint is a short planning cycle, not a promise that every uncertainty will be solved in a week. Choose work that moves your Proof of Concept (PoC) forward and produces evidence you can inspect at the next check-in. Your PoC is not expected to be complete at this stage.

## Learning Goals

By completing this assignment, you will:

- explain what has changed in your project since the mid-semester presentation;
- interpret presentation and repository feedback, deciding what to adopt, adapt, investigate, or decline;
- identify a meaningful near-term outcome that advances your PoC;
- organize a small set of actionable tasks as GitHub Issues and track them on your Project board;
- define how you will recognize progress at the end of the sprint.

## Part 1 — Prepare a Progress Check-In

Before class, gather a concise update supported by visible project evidence. Be ready to share:

1. **Current direction:** What is the project, and what are you currently trying to demonstrate in the PoC?
2. **Recent progress:** What changed or was made since your last presentation or check-in? Show the artifact, test, design, code, writing, or repository update.
3. **Feedback response:** Identify one or more useful comments from your presentation evaluation or repository review. For each, say whether you will adopt it, adapt it, investigate it, or decline it, and why.
4. **Current obstacle or uncertainty:** What question, dependency, or decision is most likely to affect your next step?
5. **Next-week outcome:** What concrete result do you expect to have by the next class?

You do not need to reproduce private evaluation forms in your public repository. Record the relevant takeaway and your response; omit personal information or comments that are not appropriate to share.

Examples of suitable progress evidence include a portion of a script and a branching map, alternative interface wireframes, a sketched sound-interaction design and a small sound-trigger test, or a revision made in response to repository feedback. These are examples, not separate requirements for every project.

## Part 2 — Plan a One-Week Sprint

In class, we will introduce the idea of a sprint as a short, focused cycle of work. Use the following lightweight structure; you do not need to adopt every formal Agile practice or vocabulary.

### 1. Set a sprint goal

Write one sentence describing the most useful outcome you can reasonably reach before the next class. Prefer a demonstrable or reviewable result over a vague activity such as “keep working on the project.”

### 2. Select a small set of tasks

Choose a manageable set of work that supports the goal. Create or update GitHub Issues so that each task:

- describes one actionable piece of work;
- has a clear completion check or evidence of done;
- is small enough to make progress on within the week, or is explicitly divided into smaller tasks;
- is connected to its real dependency, if another task must happen first.

Tasks may involve making, designing, testing, researching, writing, or revising documentation. Include time for the work that fits your project; do not create issues just to fill a prescribed count.

### 3. Track the sprint

Add the sprint issues to your GitHub Project and make sure their status reflects the work ahead. If your board supports a sprint or iteration field, you may use it; otherwise, use the board's existing workflow. Keep the sprint goal visible in the board or in a linked issue.

### 4. Define the check-in evidence

For each task, decide what you can show or point to next week to verify progress. This might be a working interaction, a test result, a wireframe reviewed by someone else, a completed section of a script, a documented decision, or an updated project artifact.

## Part 3 — Run and Review the Sprint

After the Week 10 class, work toward the sprint goal and update Issue statuses as the work progresses. If a task proves too large, blocked, or no longer useful, record what changed and revise the plan rather than silently leaving the board out of date.

Before the Week 11 check-in:

- record what you completed and link the resulting evidence;
- note what remains unfinished or blocked and why;
- write one brief reflection on what you learned and what you will do next.

## Part 4 — Update Your Project Record

Keep the work discoverable from your project repository:

- update the relevant project documentation or development log with progress and feedback decisions;
- create or revise the sprint Issues and their completion checks;
- add the sprint work to your Project board and update statuses as work proceeds;
- link new evidence from the README or the appropriate project documentation.

Use your existing repository structure where possible. Do not duplicate material that is already clear and current.

## Deliverables

By the Week 11 class meeting, your repository and Project should show:

1. **Progress evidence:** At least one current artifact or result showing meaningful project development.
2. **Feedback response:** A brief record of relevant feedback, the decision you made, and the reason.
3. **Sprint goal:** One focused outcome for the coming week.
4. **Actionable sprint Issues:** Issues with completion checks, organized on the Project board and linked to dependencies when relevant.
5. **Sprint review:** A brief record of completed, unfinished, or blocked work, with links to evidence and a next step.
6. **Next check-in evidence:** A clear indication of what you will show to demonstrate progress toward the sprint goal.

Be prepared to give a short progress update and walk through your sprint plan in class. The sprint is a planning and learning tool; it is not graded by how many Issues you close or whether an uncertain experiment succeeds.

## Completion Check

Before the next class, ask yourself:

- Can another person understand what I am trying to demonstrate in my PoC?
- Did I make a reasoned decision about feedback rather than merely collect it?
- Does my sprint goal describe a result I can review?
- Can each sprint Issue be acted on and checked for completion?
- Is my plan visible in my repository and Project board?
